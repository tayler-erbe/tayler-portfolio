Choosing a Local Model:
Find, Evaluate, Validate,
Fine-Tune

Model names are full of numbers that sound like a ranking: 8B, 27B, 32B, 35B-A3B, Q4, AWQ. They are not a ranking. This guide explains what those numbers mean, how today's models are trained, why a smaller model can beat a bigger one on a real task, and the method I use to pick, validate and, only when needed, fine-tune a model. Every recommendation here comes from evaluations I ran on one GPU, written up in my generation model evaluation and document intelligence evaluation.

For
Teams choosing an open-weight model for a specific job
Covers
Parameters · training · quantisation · benchmarks · fine-tuning · validation
Evidence
Two pre-registered evaluations on one RTX 5090
01

Reading a Model's Name

You seeIt meansWhat it does and does not tell you
27B, 32BBillions of parameters: the numbers the model learned in trainingCapacity, like the size of a library. Not how well the books were chosen, or whether the model follows instructions.
35B-A3BMixture of experts: 35B parameters in total, about 3B active for each wordFast, because most of the model sits idle on any one word. Each word gets less of the model's effort than in a dense model of the same size.
Q4, Q4_K_M, AWQCompression: each parameter stored in about 4 bits instead of 16Lets a large model fit on one card. Different methods lose different things, so the same model can behave differently under each.
Instruct, chatA version trained to follow requestsThe version you want for applications. A "base" model only continues text.
Thinking, reasoningA version that writes hidden reasoning before answeringCan help hard problems. It spends tokens and time, and under a token budget it can run out before it answers.
Version, release dateWhich training recipe produced itOften matters more than size. A newer generation can beat an older, larger one.
02

How Today's Models Are Trained

Most open-weight chat models go through three broad stages. Vendors publish different amounts of detail about each, so treat this as the common shape, not any one model's recipe.

01
Pretraining: learn language and knowledge
The model reads trillions of words of web text, books, code and other sources and learns to predict the next word. Knowledge, fluency and general reasoning come mostly from here, and this is where more parameters help most.
02
Instruction tuning: learn to follow requests
The model is trained on many examples of an instruction paired with a good answer. This turns a text predictor into an assistant. The quality and variety of these examples shape how precisely it follows instructions.
03
Preference and reinforcement tuning: learn what "better" means
The model is rewarded for answers that people, a reward model or an automatic checker prefer. Checkers that can verify an answer, such as "used exactly three bullets" or "the code passes its tests", train rule-following directly. Newer models generally put more effort into this stage.
Size mostly comes from stage 1; discipline mostly comes from stages 2 and 3. If your task needs knowledge, size helps. If it needs exact compliance with many rules at once, the recency and quality of the later stages matter more.
03

Why a Smaller Model Can Win

In my generation evaluation, Qwen3.8 27B kept 7 of 20 test groups through a strict writing generator's checks. The larger Qwen3-32B it would replace kept 3. The task needed almost no knowledge. It needed a model that obeys many rules at once: use exact wording, never copy one claim into another, never claim one thing causes another.

The run records showed how. The smaller model's plans were about a third shorter, it copied between claims in 2 groups instead of 7, and it made no cause-and-effect leaps. It needed 27 plan attempts instead of 41 and a quarter of the output tokens per accepted group. It is a later release of the same family, and the likeliest reason is newer instruction and preference training. That cannot be confirmed from outside the model, which is exactly why the test matters more than the explanation.

Four reasons a smaller model can beat a bigger one

Newer training. A later recipe can outweigh extra parameters. Concision. Under strict checks, every extra sentence is another chance to break a rule. Serving. Compression and the server change behaviour; the same 32B kept 3 groups under one setup and 1 under another. Fit. A model that fits fully on the card at the context you need beats one that spills to the CPU.

And why it might not

The same smaller model was worse on one rule: it slid from behaviour into feelings more often. A new model trades one set of failures for another. Only your own checks tell you whether the trade is worth it.

04

What Public Benchmarks Can and Cannot Tell You

Use them to build a shortlist

Public leaderboards and benchmarks such as IFEval, for instruction-following, are the cheapest way to find candidates that fit your hardware and are broadly capable. They are measured the same way for every model, so they are fair as far as they go.

Never use them as the verdict

In my evaluation, Gemma 4 had the best IFEval score by a clear margin, 0.900 against 0.830, and kept 0 of 20 groups on the real task. Qwen3.8 had the lowest IFEval score of the four models completed, and kept the most. A benchmark measures its own instructions, not yours.

05

A Six-Step Method for Picking a Model

01
Find candidates
Start from what fits your hardware at the context length you need, then shortlist from public benchmarks, recent releases and the families you already run. Include your current model as the baseline.
02
Turn your real task into a test
Freeze a set of real cases with automatic, code-based checks or answer keys. No model grades another. Keep failed and timed-out answers in the denominator.
03
Write the decision rule before you run
State what counts as better, by how much, and what you will do with a pass. Freeze it by hash. With several candidates and small samples, one will look good by luck; a rule written first keeps you honest.
04
Prove the test is faithful
Your current model must reproduce its known result inside the harness. If it does not, you are measuring the harness, and nothing is concluded.
05
Change one thing at a time
Run the same model on each server and compression you are considering, so serving effects are not mistaken for model effects. Test prompt changes and model changes separately, or in a two-by-two grid.
06
Confirm on data it has not seen
A screening win earns a confirmation run on new cases and a new random seed, with an interval on the difference. Adopt only after that, through your normal release checks.
06

When and How to Fine-Tune

Fine-tuning means training an existing model further on your own examples. It is the most expensive lever, so try it last.

OrderLeverCostTry it when
1Fix the instructionsMinutesAlways first. Look for contradictions: my generator's prompt banned a phrase that its own anchor rule required.
2Switch or route modelsHours of evaluationA candidate wins your pre-registered test. Consider one model per stage when models fail at different stages.
3Fine-tuneDays, plus GPU timeThe best prompt and model together still miss your bar, and you can produce enough good examples.
Rejection-sampling fine-tuning

If your task has automatic checks, you already have a free labeller. Generate many outputs, keep only those that pass every check, and train the model on them. It learns to produce passing outputs more often on the first try.

Preference tuning

Pair a passing and a failing answer to the same request, and train the model to prefer the passing one. It teaches the difference directly, not just the good example.

LoRA and QLoRA: fine-tuning on one GPU

Instead of changing all of a model's parameters, LoRA trains a small add-on, usually well under one percent of the model's size. QLoRA does the same on a compressed 4-bit copy of the base model, which is what can make a 27B model trainable on a single 32 GB card. You keep the add-on as a small separate file and load it on top of the base model.

The main risk: learning the checks, not the job

A model trained to pass checks can learn to pass them while writing worse. Hold out cases it never trains on, and measure general skill and quality alongside the checks.

07

How to Validate a Tuned Model

01
Split by case, before you train
Training, validation and held-out test sets are drawn as whole cases, never as rows of the same case, and the held-out set is never seen during training or tuning.
02
Compare like with like
Run the tuned and untuned model on the same server, compression, prompt and seeds, so the only difference is the training.
03
Guard what the checks cannot see
Re-run a public benchmark to catch lost general skill, and an independent quality read of a fixed sample to catch outputs that pass the rules but read worse. Each guard has a limit you state in advance.
04
Then release it like any other change
A tuned model that wins still goes through your normal release route: a small live trial, a quality review and a way back.
