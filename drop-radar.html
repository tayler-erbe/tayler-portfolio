Drop Radar
A drop-alert system that learns from his texts

Drop Radar is a real-time alert system I built for my husband over one weekend. It watches about 40 stores and sources around the clock for the limited releases he collects, pings his phone within seconds when one goes live, and improves itself from the notes he texts it. It only ever alerts. He still makes every purchase himself.

01 / Why I built it

Why I built it

My husband worked at a card shop when he was 12. He went on to a completely different career, but he never stopped loving collecting and reselling: trading cards, collectible toys, watches.

For months he was glued to his phone trying to catch limited drops, and he kept losing them to bots. These releases sell out in minutes, and no person can refresh a dozen stores at once while also living their life.

So I built Drop Radar in a weekend. The goal was simple. He could stop watching his phone, trust it to tell him when something mattered, and we would get that time back together.

A few days in, its AI deep search found a limited-edition watch, only 100 made, that sold out within minutes. He got one.

02 / What it does

What it does

01
Watches about 40 sources, all day and night
Store product feeds for new items and restocks, store homepages for a waiting room appearing (a queue going up means a drop is starting), page and keyword watchers, and RSS feeds. Each source has its own check interval, and checks tighten inside the drop windows he knows well, like weekday mornings at one store and evenings at another. On top of that, Discord restock channels arrive over the official live connection, and an AI deep search hunts for small-batch limited editions the scripted feeds miss.
02
Filters to what he actually wants
Online, at retail price, from the real store. Third-party marketplace sellers are flagged, and in-store sightings, resale listings and giveaways go to the dashboard instead of his phone. The same product or link only alerts once per 30 minutes across all sources, and quiet hours keep the night quiet.
03
Alerts at three levels
LIVE means buyable now or the queue is up, with a loud push. SOON is an announcement, with a normal push. INFO goes to the dashboard only. Alerts go to a Telegram bot, with optional Pushover critical alerts and ntfy, and carry the product, store, price, seller, when it was posted, when it was seen, and the direct store link.
04
Gets him to checkout faster, within store rules
One-tap cart links that open checkout with the item already in the cart (Drop Radar builds the link and never opens it). Reminders 24 hours and 15 minutes before an announced drop, with time zones converted and tested across a daylight-saving change. A per-store checklist so his accounts are ready for invite and raffle programs.
05
Keeps a private ledger of his collection
It reads a dedicated inbox, read-only, every 15 minutes and parses order, shipping, delivery and cancellation emails from about 15 retailers, plus eBay seller emails. From those it keeps cost basis, what he still holds, going rates with their source and date, days held, and a SELL NOW, HOLD or WATCH suggestion with its reason. For eBay it works out fees, payout and profit and reminds him 24 and 4 hours before a ship-by deadline. Emails it cannot read go to a review list, never dropped. All of it stays on our home PC.
03 / The self-improving loop

It improves itself at his command

This is my favourite part. The Telegram bot is not only an alert channel. It is a two-way line to an AI agent. Commands like /add, /remove, /status, /remind, /ledger and /price run straight away. Anything else he sends, a text, a dictated note or a screenshot, is saved as feedback.

A file watcher notices the new note (or a source that has started failing) and queues a run of a dedicated Claude Code agent. The agent has written rules and a living knowledge file. It reads the note, works out what is wrong, edits the code or the source list, runs the tests, restarts the service, and replies on Telegram with what it changed. He never touches a line of code.

The agent also runs on a schedule: a daily review, a weekly review, a weekly resale-picks briefing and a monthly research pass. Every weekly review ends with a feedback check-in. Each change from that week gets Keep, Change and Drop buttons, plus one open question that rotates, so the person using it checks the system every week.

He asked for it to be easy, so questions come as tap-to-choose Telegram buttons rather than paragraphs: what to follow, which Discord servers, how often the deep search runs. Every choice is saved to a file the agent acts on, and every choice can be undone. Each extra feature also has a live on and off switch, so anything he declines comes out without a restart.

04 / Architecture and stack

Architecture and stack

Everything is standard-library Python, run by Windows Task Scheduler with a wrapper that restarts it if it stops. No frameworks, no proxies, nothing to install beyond Python itself.

Language
Python, standard library only: urllib, sqlite3, imaplib, email, threading, http.server, and a websocket client built on ssl and socket
Alerts and chat
Telegram Bot API with inline keyboards, Pushover, ntfy
Ingestion
Shopify product feeds, waiting-room pages, page and keyword watchers, RSS, Discord REST and Gateway APIs, email over IMAP (read-only)
AI
A Claude Code subagent that maintains the system, scheduled agent runs through an autonomous job queue, Claude web research for the deep search, and ChatGPT as an independent second opinion
Running it
Windows Task Scheduler with an auto-restart wrapper, a GitHub Pages dashboard, unittest

Discord, the official way. I joined nine community restock servers for him and followed six of their announcement channels into his own private server, where a read-only bot reads the posts. It started as REST polling every 10 seconds. I moved it to Discord’s official Gateway through a websocket client written on the standard library, with heartbeat, resume and dead-connection detection, so posts now arrive in about a second, with REST polling as an automatic fallback. No self-bots, no user tokens.

Health that tells the truth. Every source has a health row with real states. The Discord feed only counts as healthy when a followed post actually arrived in the last 24 hours, not just because the code ran. An arrival log records every alert’s upstream post time, detection time and send time, and feeds a monthly summary of which sources are fastest and each store’s busiest day and hour.

Bugs found in live data. A backoff that froze the whole loop for up to an hour (a blocked host is now skipped, not slept on). Channels whose first posts were swallowed because they had no baseline yet. A substring match that let “atc” fire on “watch”. A feedback watcher that queued notes it had already handled again. All four were found in live data and fixed.

06 / What it will not do

What it will not do

Alerts only

Drop Radar never buys, never joins or skips a queue, never solves a CAPTCHA and never tries to get around bot protection. It uses no proxies. He still makes every purchase himself.

Checked the rules first

I researched all 22 stores he buys from: the platform, the bot protection I could observe, and their terms of service, quoted and dated. None permits automated checkout, so I built the legitimate speed-ups instead: cart links, reminders and account readiness.

No scraping where it is forbidden

TCGplayer and eBay forbid scraping, and eBay’s sold-price API is restricted. Going rates are automated only through a paid API that permits it, and otherwise flagged for him to check by hand.

Polite to every store

When a store pushes back with a 403 or 429, Drop Radar checks it less often. Hosts are spaced out. It never hides behind proxies.

His data stays home

Every purchase, sale and timing record stays on our home PC and never goes into the code repository. Git ignores it twice over, and a test proves it.

08 / What I learned

What I learned

The person using it runs the roadmap

He judged the early alerts low-value, and he was right. That reset the design within a day. It is also why the weekly check-in is built in, not bolted on.

An AI that goes looking finds different things

Feeds wait for a store to post. The deep search goes hunting for small-batch editions, and its first run found the watch.

Healthy means it worked, not that it ran

A source that runs without errors but never delivers anything is broken. Health now checks for real arrivals.

Rules first, then speed

Reading every store’s terms before building meant every speed-up I shipped is one he can use without putting his accounts at risk.

I also ran the big decisions past ChatGPT as an outside consultant, including a ranked top-10 plan for earlier alerts and better checkout odds under the no-bot rules, and adopted its strongest free ideas the same day.
