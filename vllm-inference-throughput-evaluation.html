01 / Summary

Executive Summary

This case study documents two controlled benchmarks of the Illinois Legislation extraction pipeline on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU (22.5 GB, 72W TDP). The first sweep characterized three open-weight 7-8B AWQ-quantized models across six chunk sizes; the second characterized the same models' scaling behavior across six concurrency levels. Total: 108 cells, 162 measurements, 14.4 GPU-hours unattended, zero safety alarms, < 0.5% cross-repeat variance.

Model recommendation: Llama 3.1 8B AWQ at chunk_size=1,200 tokens, concurrency=12-24. It is the quality champion (91-92.5% parse OK across all 18 cells) and the only model whose tail latency stays bounded under concurrency (39s p99 at conc=24 vs Mistral's 74s). Quality does not degrade as concurrency increases — verified across all three models. The incumbent (Mistral 7B at chunk_size=1,200) is dominated on both axes by Llama 3.1 and should be replaced.

Production SLO finding: A single L4 cannot meet the national-scale multi-state SLO (75,000 bills/week in an 18-hour processing window). Measured against the real 11,079-bill Illinois corpus distribution — not the controlled eval sample — the shortfall at Llama 3.1 quality is 3.16× for steady-state and 6.57× for peak-burst. This is the engineering requirement the new hardware must close: ~3 L4s in parallel (or one A100-class GPU) for steady, ~7 L4s (or H100-class) for peak-burst.

What this work delivers beyond the model choice: a hardware-agnostic, reproducible evaluation methodology with per-request telemetry, defensive automation, and trap-based recovery. Whatever the new server turns out to be, the same 108-cell sweep ports directly and gives a measured before/after comparison — making the new hardware's value quantifiable rather than asserted.

02 / Problem

The Business Problem

Our production legislation pipeline ingests Illinois bills, chunks them, and runs LLM extraction to populate 15 structured fields used by downstream analysts and policy researchers. It had been running on Ollama with Mistral 7B on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU on an internal server. Three pressures motivated re-evaluation.

Resource utilization. Production GPU metrics showed Ollama exercising only about 28% of the L4's compute. We were paying for hardware we weren't using, and the headroom became impossible to ignore as we started planning national-scale expansion.

Throughput ceiling. For a 50-state rollout, we projected needing roughly 250 tokens/sec sustained and 650 tokens/sec at peak. Ollama wasn't close. We needed continuous batching, not single-stream serial inference.

Newer model options. Two open-weight models — Qwen 2.5 7B and Llama 3.1 8B — had landed since the original Mistral deployment. If either extracted noticeably better on our 15-field schema, the upgrade was free.

The goal was to defend or replace the production decision before committing GPU time to a backfill that would take days. The wrong answer here would mean weeks of regenerating bad data.

03 / Method

Methodology

Controlled-Variable Design

The hardest part of LLM evaluation is keeping the comparison fair. We made five design choices to enforce this.

50-bill controlled sample. All test bills are 4,500 to 4,972 real Mistral tokens — chosen to sit at the production P95 of bill length without exceeding any model's prompt budget at chunk_size=3000. This keeps chunk count comparable across profiles, so cross-cell differences reflect model and chunk-size behavior, not different input distributions.

Three models in the sweep:

  • Mistral 7B Instruct v0.3 AWQ (production incumbent)
  • Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct AWQ (newer challenger, optimized for instruction-following)
  • Llama 3.1 8B Instruct AWQ (newer challenger, roughly 14% more parameters)

Six chunk sizes: 500, 700, 900, 1200, 2000, 3000 tokens. Covers the full reasonable range for our extraction prompt without entering territory where the prompt template itself dominates.

Three repeats per cell. Lets us distinguish run-to-run variance from genuine signal. With 18 profiles × 3 repeats we have 54 cells total.

Concurrency = 4. Realistic production concurrency. Each cell exercised vLLM's continuous batching at 4 in-flight requests, as the pipeline actually runs.

vLLM 0.6.6 with AWQ-Marlin quantization, --max-model-len 8192, prefix caching enabled. The 8192 ceiling was sized from real prompt math: 3000-token chunks + ~1500-token prompt template + 1024-token completion budget + safety margin.

Instrumentation

We built per-request telemetry into the vLLM client — TTFT, ITL p50/p99, decode throughput, prefill throughput, prompt and completion token counts — and a server-side perf monitor sampling GPU state at 10-second intervals across the full sweep. A safety alarm framework would have halted the sweep automatically if temperature crossed 85°C, KV cache exceeded 99%, or memory exceeded 95% (sustained 60 seconds, to ignore transients). None did.

Every claim in this report is supported by either 4,586 individual per-request samples or 3,082 server-side time-series samples. Both datasets are available in the appendix.

04 / Quality

Quality Results

The 15 extraction fields range from easy (one-sentence summarization tasks like policy_domain) to hard (multi-sentence reasoning tasks like ideological_alignment and original_law). A parse is "OK" if all 15 fields receive non-empty, well-formed values; "PARTIAL" if any field is missing.

Fig 1   Each point represents one (model × chunk_size) cell, averaged across 3 repeats. Llama 3.1 forms a distinct cluster in the upper region — 85% to 92.5% parse OK across every chunk size. Qwen forms a cluster on the right (high throughput, mediocre quality). Mistral underperforms on both axes. The dashed ring marks llama31_500, the composite winner.

Headline Finding

All six Llama 3.1 cells occupy the top six quality slots. Every Llama 3.1 cell beats every Mistral and Qwen cell on parse OK rate. The choice of model dominates the choice of chunk size — and the composite winner sits on the Pareto frontier.

Fig 2   Llama 3.1's quality is nearly flat across chunk sizes (85-92.5%, a 7.5-point spread). Mistral climbs steeply with chunk size (56% to 72%, a 16-point spread). Qwen is flat-low with an unexplained dip at chunk_size=1200. The operational implication: with Llama 3.1 you can pick whatever chunk size is convenient. With Mistral you have to tune.

Fig 3   Qwen wins throughput at every chunk size, by a ~30-50% margin over the others. Mistral and Llama 3.1 are nearly identical on throughput. The question isn't whether Llama 3.1 is slow, it's why Qwen is unusually fast. Chart 8 will explain.

Fig 4   Per-field success rate (% of chunks where the field has a non-empty value), restricted to each model's winning composite profile at chunk_size=500. Green ≥95%, yellow 85-95%, orange 70-85%, red below 70%.

The Most Diagnostic Single Chart in This Report

Easy fields (top of chart) sit at 100% for all three models. The hard fields — ideological_alignment, original_law, decreasing_aspects — are where the models separate dramatically. Llama 3.1 handles ideological_alignment at 98% while Mistral drops to 71% and Qwen to 78%. This 27-point gap predicts the overall composite ranking almost by itself. Llama 3.1 is the only model that handles all 15 fields above 91%.

05 / Performance

Performance Results

Charts 1-4 answered which model is best. Charts 5-12 explain why, and reveal two performance findings that change the production story.

Fig 5   TTFT scales linearly with prompt length across all three models — the textbook signature of prefill-bound startup. The ~5× spread from chunk_size=500 (~0.18s) to 3000 (~0.8s) tracks the ~6× spread in prompt tokens. Qwen has a slight (~10-15%) prefill edge.

Fig 6   Per-stream decode throughput at concurrency=4. The single-stream smoke-test baseline was 53.5 tokens/sec; under concurrency=4 each stream gets roughly 70-85% of that. Mistral has a small per-stream advantage, but per-stream rate doesn't determine total throughput — total completion-tokens-per-second does, and that's where Qwen wins via verbosity (Chart 8).

Fig 7   Inter-token latency at the p99 percentile. This chart reveals the hidden cost of Qwen's apparent throughput advantage.

Major Hidden Finding

Mistral stays tightly bounded at 36-44ms ITL p99. Llama 3.1 stays tightly bounded at 40-49ms. Qwen ranges from 65ms to 118ms — 2-3× higher tail latency variance. Mean throughput hides Qwen's spiky per-token behavior. For real-time applications with SLAs, Qwen is a worse choice than Chart 3's mean throughput numbers suggested.

Fig 8   Mean completion tokens per chunk by chunk size. The same prompt produces very different output lengths from the three models.

Why Qwen Looks Fast

Qwen produces roughly 30-40% fewer completion tokens per chunk than Mistral and Llama 3.1 (~270-310 vs ~410-465). Less verbose output means fewer tokens to decode, which means higher chunks-per-second. Qwen's throughput dominance isn't from faster inference — it's from a more concise model. Whether that's a feature or a defect depends on whether richer extractions would be useful downstream.

Fig 9   GPU utilization sampled every 10 seconds across the full 8.5-hour unattended sweep. Mean utilization during active periods: 93.7%. The three visible dips correspond to vLLM model swaps (mistral→qwen at ~2h45m, qwen→llama31 at ~5h00m). Compare to the previous Ollama deployment at ~28% mean utilization.

Fig 10   Peak temperature was 70°C, well below the 85°C alarm threshold and the 90°C thermal throttle point. The L4 had substantial thermal headroom for this workload — higher concurrency levels in the planned next experiment will not risk thermal limits.

Fig 11   Power was at or just above the L4's 72W TDP for the entire active period, peaking at 76.6W. This is the correct power profile for compute-bound inference serving — the GPU is busy doing matrix multiplies, not waiting on memory.

Fig 12   KV cache utilization stayed below 12% throughout the entire sweep. The spikes at hours 2-3 and 8 correspond to 3000-token chunk cells — larger chunks consume more cache. The alarm threshold of 99% was never approached. This is the empirical evidence that justifies higher concurrency in the next experiment: we have roughly 8× more KV cache headroom than we used.

07 / Lessons

What We Learned (Honestly)

The Config-Drift Bug

Two cells (mistral_1200 and qwen_1200) in the main sweep produced zero output. The harness reported Extractor: SUCCESS, extracted=0, failed=201 — a deceptively cheerful status given that nothing had actually worked.

Root cause: Config files generated months earlier by an older tooling version had backend: ollama hardcoded. They were syntactically valid YAML, semantically wrong for the current stack. The sweep ran the extractor with the wrong backend, got 201 immediate connection failures, and exited in 3 seconds per cell looking successful.

Caught how: Post-sweep aggregation flagged composite=0, chunks/sec=0, wall=3s for those two cells. We diffed the broken configs against the working _900 configs in the same family, saw the backend: field discrepancy, cloned the working configs, and re-ran the 6 affected cells in 60 minutes. No data was lost.

Pre-flight check added for future sweeps: grep "backend:" configs/extractor_*.yaml | sort | uniq -c. Any config that disagrees with its siblings is suspect.

Config files generated by older tooling can survive into newer infrastructure with no syntactic complaint. The system succeeds at producing nothing.

Defensive Automation Paid Off Five Separate Times

Five distinct bugs were caught before they could ruin the sweep:

  1. --max-model-len 4096 would have caused OOM at chunk_size=3000.
  2. ps -eo truncates the cmd column, which broke perf_monitor's process-detection regex.
  3. The vLLM-env Python doesn't have pandas; the harness has to use system Python.
  4. A patch anchor was ambiguous (3 matches in the target file) and would have corrupted source code.
  5. The eval manifest contained 1 bill instead of 50; the smoke test passed on bad data, so we caught it by reading the actual manifest before launching the real sweep.

Each was caught by a deliberate verification step, not by running the sweep and seeing it fail. Cheap verification before expensive computation is the rule that pays.

Estimates vs Reality

Expected sweep wall time: 22-26 hours. Actual: 8h 34m — 3× faster than projected. Reasons: pre-generated chunks (no chunker delay during runtime), smaller chunk sizes finished much faster than estimated, and vLLM startup cost was paid only 3 times (once per model) instead of 18.

08 / Experiment 2

Concurrency Sweep — Finding the Knee

Experiment 1 answered which model. Experiment 2 answered at what concurrency. The first sweep ran at concurrency=4 across all 18 chunk-size cells; that established the model ranking but said nothing about scaling. To recommend a production operating point we needed empirical throughput curves — how much speedup each model delivers as we add concurrent requests, where the diminishing-returns knee sits, and whether quality degrades under load.

Design

Three models at each one's quality-best chunk size, six concurrency levels, three repeats per cell. Same 50-bill controlled sample, same warm-start protocol, same defensive trap-based shutdown:

Profilechunk_sizeReason for inclusion
llama31_12001,200 tokensQuality champion from Exp 1 (92.5% parse OK)
qwen_500500 tokensThroughput champion candidate (most chunks per bill, but fastest per chunk)
mistral_30003,000 tokensIncumbent baseline at its quality-best chunk size (72% OK)

Concurrency levels: 1, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24. The conc=4 cells double as a reproducibility check against Experiment 1 — same model, same chunk size, same hardware, different day.

Total: 3 × 6 × 3 = 54 cells, projected ~10 GPU-hours. Actual: 5h 54m unattended for the first run + 3h 06m for a targeted recovery run = 9h 00m (more on the recovery below).

The Path Bug and Why Recovery Worked

The sweep launched at 16:50 on Thursday May 21. Mistral and Qwen completed all 36 of their cells cleanly. At 22:41 the script attempted to load Llama 3.1 and hit a vLLM startup failure within 180 seconds — the sweep's safety timeout fired, the trap cleaned up, vLLM stopped, GPU returned to idle, and the master log recorded:

HFValidationError: Repo id must be in the form 'repo_name' or 'namespace/repo_name':
'/var/advanalytics/datashare/hf_models/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-AWQ'

Root cause: I had typed the wrong model directory in the sweep script. The actual path was Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-AWQ-INT4. Exp 1's working script used the correct path; I failed to copy-paste it when writing Exp 2. The pre-flight should have included an ls of every model path against actual disk. Lesson logged.

The recovery was straightforward because the original sweep had failed in exactly the right way:

  • The 180-second timeout caught the startup failure before it consumed GPU time
  • The trap-based cleanup left no orphan processes and no GPU lock
  • The 36 completed cells were untouched — pristine, repeatable data
  • The master log named the failing model and the specific error

I corrected the path, wrote a targeted recovery script that ran only the 18 missing Llama 3.1 cells against the same benchmarks directory, and launched at 09:42 the next morning. It completed at 12:48 with all 18 cells passing. Total recovery overhead: ~10 minutes of human time (diagnose, write, launch). The methodology held.

Throughput Scaling — What the GPU Actually Does

Latency Tail — Where the Queue Wins

Throughput is what you advertise; tail latency is what gets you paged at 2 AM. The p99 distribution tells a sharper story than the means:

The Mistral cliff between conc=4 and conc=8 (24s → 43s) is the moment the L4's KV cache starts contending for space. Mistral's 3000-token chunks demand more cache than the other profiles, and once the scheduler starts preempting, p99 reflects the queue waiting time, not the model's actual decode time.

Quality Under Load — The Production-Defining Finding

This finding eliminates an entire class of risk from the production decision. Concurrency tuning is a hardware-utilization problem, not a quality-tradeoff problem. The right operating point for any model is the one that maximizes throughput within latency SLO — no need to budget quality headroom against queue depth.

Reproducibility

Three repeats per cell, 54 cells, 162 individual benchmark runs. Maximum variance across repeats of the same cell:

  • Throughput: < 0.5% (e.g. Mistral c=4 across reps: 0.271 / 0.271 / 0.271 chunks/sec)
  • p99 latency: < 1.5% (e.g. Llama c=24 reps: 39.39s / 38.95s / 39.11s)
  • Quality: < 0.3 percentage points (most cells: identical across all three reps)

This is what controlled-variable benchmarking looks like when the harness is right. The numbers in this case study are not estimates or extrapolations; they are measurements with quantified noise floors. Any subsequent test on different hardware can be compared cell-by-cell against this baseline.

09 / SLO Gate

Production SLO Gate — The Honest Answer

The experiments above characterize the model+hardware combination under controlled conditions. They do not, on their own, tell you whether the system meets production requirements. A senior performance engineer treats those as two distinct measurements with two distinct framings, and refuses to confuse the two.

The production SLO is defined by the workload contract, not by what we happened to measure:

Multi-state production workload contract: Process ~75,000 bills/week (50 states × 1,500 bills/wk peak coverage) within a 3 nights × 6 hours = 18 hours/week processing window. Required throughput: 1.157 bills/second sustained, or 2.41 bills/second during peak-burst weeks. SLO is met if measured p99 throughput clears the requirement.

Why the Eval Sample Cannot Be the SLO Test

The 50-bill eval sample used in both experiments is intentionally uniform — every bill is 4,500–5,000 tokens (Mistral tokenizer). That tight distribution is what gives the controlled-variable comparison its statistical power. But it also means the sample stresses the top quartile of the production bill-length distribution. Using it directly for an SLO calculation overstates the chunking workload by a factor of 1.4× to 3.4× depending on profile, and produces a misleading "0 of 18 operating points pass" verdict that is technically true but practically wrong.

The right way to compute the SLO is to use the real production corpus distribution:

What This Means

  • The eval is not obsolete; it's the comparison baseline. When new hardware arrives, the same 108-cell methodology ports directly and the new measurements compare cell-by-cell against these numbers.
  • The hardware gap is precise. The new server must deliver approximately 3× the per-GPU throughput of an L4 to meet steady-state SLO at Llama 3.1 quality, or ~6.6× for peak-burst capacity. That's a measured engineering requirement, not a guess.
  • Llama 3.1 is the production choice regardless of what the new server is. It dominates Mistral on both axes (quality and tail latency) and dominates Qwen on quality by 29 percentage points. Mistral as the incumbent has been the wrong choice.
10 / What's Next

What's Next

Experiment 2 (concurrency sweep) is documented above. The remaining pipeline of follow-up experiments addresses the questions that this work cannot answer on its own — and which become more important now that we have a defensible production model recommendation and a quantified hardware gap.

Experiment 3 — Human-Graded Quality Validation

Parse-OK is a structural-validity check — it tells us the model produced the right shape of JSON, but not whether the extracted values are correct. Experiment 3 replaces that with blind human grading of accuracy, specificity, and completeness on a stratified sample of the operating points Exp 2 identified (specifically Llama 3.1 at concurrency=12, 16, and 24). The deliverable is a per-field grade matrix that confirms the structural quality scores reflect substantive quality.

This experiment is GPU-free (data is already generated). Estimated effort: ~3 days of human review.

Experiment 4 — Larger Parameter Models

The three models tested here are all 7-8 billion parameters. The hypothesis worth testing is whether parameter scaling (with appropriate quantization) closes the quality-throughput-cost tradeoff in a meaningful way for legislative extraction specifically. The candidate models:

  • Qwen 2.5 32B AWQ — same model family as the Qwen 2.5 tested here, 4× the parameters, native 128K context window. Hypothesis: parameter scaling lifts quality without proportional throughput loss because AWQ keeps the model in a single GPU.
  • Llama 3.3 70B AWQ — quality ceiling test. Requires > 22 GB VRAM, so it cannot run on the current L4. Belongs in the post-hardware-upgrade evaluation.

This experiment requires the new server — Qwen 2.5 32B at INT4 quantization needs ~20–22 GB working VRAM plus KV cache, which is right at the L4's ceiling. The experiment is the first justified use of the new hardware after it lands.

Experiment 5 — Production-Scale Validation

The 50-bill controlled sample answers the question "how do these models compare on a stress-test distribution?" The 11,079-bill production corpus is needed to answer "how does the chosen model behave on real traffic, end-to-end, across the full bill-length distribution?" Experiment 5 runs the chosen production model (Llama 3.1 8B AWQ or whatever Exp 4 changes that recommendation to) against the full Illinois corpus, at the operating concurrency identified in Exp 2, with full instrumentation. The deliverable is a production cost model: total chunks, total tokens, total GPU-hours, total cost-equivalent at managed-service pricing for comparison.

Experiment 6 (Optional) — NVIDIA Nemotron Comparison

Nemotron was raised in early scoping conversations as a potentially superior model. The variant currently available on the urbaicompute1 server is not a direct fit for our schema-constrained extraction prompt, and dropping it into the current sweep would mix too many variables (different hardware, different serving stack, different prompt fit). The right way to evaluate it is a dedicated experiment with comparable methodology after Experiments 3–5 establish a clean baseline. The result either confirms the open-weight stack as superior for this workload, or identifies Nemotron as the production target on the new hardware. Either answer is useful.
